How EEG Filtering Works

(Translated by Claude Opus 5.5)

Our preprocessing includes a "0.1–30 Hz filter" step. This page explains three things: which filter is actually used, why it was chosen, and what filtering actually computes mathematically. All four figures are interactive.

1How we actually filter

In MNE the most common way to write it is a single line:

raw.filter(l_freq=0.1, h_freq=30)

When no other parameters are given, MNE uses the following defaults:

ItemMNE defaultMeaning
TypeFIR band-pass, doing the high-pass and low-pass in one goConvolution with a single fixed filter kernel (Section 4)
Kernel designfirwin: the sinc function of an ideal band-pass multiplied by a Hamming windowPasses 0.1–30 Hz and blocks the other frequencies
PhaseZero-phaseThe kernel is symmetric, so the waveform is not delayed as a whole (Section 7)
Low-frequency transition band0.1 Hz wideGradually let through between 0 and 0.1 Hz; attenuated by half at 0.05 Hz
High-frequency transition band7.5 Hz wideGradually removed between 30 and 37.5 Hz; attenuated by half at about 33.75 Hz
Kernel lengthAbout 33 seconds, over thirty thousand points at 1024 HzThe narrower the low-frequency transition band, the longer the kernel must be (calculation below)

How the kernel length is calculated. For an FIR filter designed with a Hamming window, the kernel length is inversely proportional to the transition bandwidth. By default MNE sets it with this formula:

Kernel length (s) ≈ 3.3 / transition bandwidth (Hz)
PartTransition bandwidthResulting kernel length
0.1 Hz high-pass0.1 Hz3.3 ÷ 0.1 = 33 s
30 Hz low-pass7.5 Hz3.3 ÷ 7.5 ≈ 0.44 s

When the two are combined into one band-pass filter, the length is set by the longer one, so it is 33 seconds. Intuitively: to tell 0.05 Hz apart from 0.1 Hz (periods of 20 s and 10 s), you have to look at a long enough stretch of data, so the kernel is naturally long. And because this length far exceeds a 1-second epoch, filtering must be done on the whole continuous recording, before cutting epochs.

The 3.3 in the formula is not picked by feel; it is a property of the Hamming window itself: for a sinc filter windowed with it, the transition bandwidth is roughly 3.3 divided by the kernel length, while the stopband is suppressed to about two thousandths of the original (about −53 dB). With a different window the constant changes too; for example, for the Blackman window it is about 5.5, giving a wider transition but stronger stopband suppression. It is "approximately" because it is an approximate value measured from the window function's spectrum.

So 0.1 and 30 are where the transition bands begin, not hard cut points. To confirm the parameters actually used, look at the filter line in the script, or at the "Setting up band-pass filter…" message MNE prints in the terminal at run time and the FIR parameter lines below it.

2Why 0.1 Hz and 30 Hz

0.1 Hz high-pass: remove slow drift without harming the ERP

What we want to remove is drift caused by sweating, skin potentials and slowly changing electrodes; their periods are often longer than ten-odd seconds, and they are what makes the raw waveform wander up and down as a whole. The reason not to set the high-pass higher (say 1 Hz) is that too high a cutoff distorts the ERP: slow components (P3, LPP) get weakened, and a spurious wave of opposite polarity appears next to the waveform. The traditional general recommendation is 0.1–30 Hz, and ERP CORE officially uses a 0.1 Hz high-pass too [1]. A 2024 component-by-component study from the Luck lab found that the high-pass for N170 can actually be set as high as 0.9 Hz, whereas for slow components such as P3 and N400 it should not exceed 0.2 Hz [2]. So for N170, 0.1 Hz is a conservative but safe choice.

30 Hz low-pass: mainly for decoding

ReasonExplanation
30 Hz does not harm N170A study on ERP CORE data found that, for measuring N170 amplitude, a low-pass at 30 Hz or higher does not noticeably distort it; for measuring latency, a 10–20 Hz low-pass is actually better [2]. The removed high-frequency part contains 60 Hz line noise (the North American mains frequency) and some of the muscle activity (EMG). But EMG is broadband: it is most prominent at high frequencies but also extends down to quite low frequencies [3], so a low-pass can only reduce EMG, not remove it completely
Decoding classifies each time point separatelyUnlike ERP measurement, which averages over a time window, high-frequency noise directly interferes with the classifier at every time point
Must low-pass before downsamplingWe later downsample to 100 Hz. Sampling at 100 Hz can only correctly represent signals below 50 Hz; components above 50 Hz "fold" into fake low-frequency signals (aliasing, see Section 8). So a low-pass must come before downsampling, and 30 Hz leaves ample margin

ERP CORE's official preprocessing does not apply a low-pass: they measure N170 as the mean amplitude over 110–150 ms, and averaging over a time window already suppresses high frequencies; for plotting and peak measurement they additionally used a 20 Hz low-pass. Nestor's paper uses 0.1–40 Hz. Either 30 or 40 is defensible: lower is smoother with less noise but smears more in time; higher keeps more detail along with a bit more noise.

3The frequency view: multiply each frequency by a coefficient

Any signal can be decomposed into a sum of many sine waves of different frequencies (Fourier analysis):

x(t) = Σf Af sin(2πf t + φf)

x(t) is the raw EEG signal, f is frequency, Af is the amplitude of that frequency component, and φf is its phase. Filtering means multiplying the amplitude of each frequency component by a coefficient H(f), called the frequency response:

Affiltered = H(f) · Af
FilterShape of H(f)
30 Hz low-passH ≈ 1 below 30 Hz (kept), H ≈ 0 above (removed)
0.1 Hz high-passH ≈ 0 below 0.1 Hz, H ≈ 1 above
0.1–30 Hz band-passThe two multiplied together, keeping only the middle range

A real H(f) does not jump suddenly from 1 to 0 at 30 Hz; in between there is a gradually falling transition band. The narrower the transition band, the "steeper" the filter, but the longer the filter kernel must be, and the larger the side effects in time (visible in the figure in Section 5).

4The time view: convolution is a sliding weighted average

The actual computation does not need to decompose frequencies at all; instead it performs a convolution in time:

y[n] = Σk h[k] · x[n − k]

x[n] is the raw voltage at sample n, y[n] is the filtered value at sample n, and h[k] is a fixed set of weights called the filter kernel (also called the impulse response). In words: each output point = a weighted sum of a run of nearby raw points, then slide forward one step at a time.

The simplest case is a 3-point average, with h being three equal weights of 1/3. See what happens to two kinds of signal:

SignalRaw xy after 3-point averageResult
Fast jitter0, 6, 0, 6, 0, 6, 02, 4, 2, 4, 2Amplitude squeezed from 6 to 2
Slow change1, 2, 3, 4, 52, 3, 4Exactly the same as before

The fast part is shrunk and the slow part is kept: that is a low-pass. A high-pass can be thought of as "raw signal − low-passed signal", subtracting the slow part and leaving the fast part.

With a window width of 1, the output equals the raw signal; the wider it gets, the more fast jitter is averaged away and the closer the purple line hugs the dashed line. But when the window is too wide, the slow undulations themselves also get smoothed out somewhat; that is the problem of setting the cutoff frequency too low.

5What a real filter kernel looks like

A moving average is the crudest low-pass; a real filter differs in just two ways: a longer kernel (hundreds to tens of thousands of points) and unequal weights (large in the middle, small at the sides, some even negative). An H(f) designed this way can fairly accurately achieve "keep below the cutoff, remove above it". h and H(f) correspond one to one: designing a filter means first deciding the H(f) you want, then computing the corresponding h.

6Two kinds of filter: FIR and IIR

TypeHow it is computedCharacteristicsWho uses it
FIR
finite impulse response		The convolution of Section 4, using only the raw signal xStable, zero-phase is easy; but the kernel is very long when the cutoff is lowMNE default
IIR
infinite impulse response		Also uses outputs already computed: y[n] = Σ bkx[n−k] − Σ aky[n−k]Few coefficients, fast, can be very steep; distorts if poorly designedThe Butterworth used by ERP CORE

In the IIR formula, bk are the coefficients applied to the raw input and ak are the coefficients applied to previous outputs. Because the output "feeds back" in, the influence of one input in theory lasts forever, hence "infinite".

7Zero-phase: no delay, but it "smears" backward in time

A filter that uses only past and present points (a causal filter) delays the whole output, and the ERP peaks become later too. The zero-phase approach makes the filter kernel symmetric (or filters once forward and then once backward), so there is no delay and peak positions stay put. The cost: computing y[n] also uses points after n, so the filtered output starts changing before the signal actually appears.

Causal filtering delays far too much, so ERP research basically always uses zero-phase.

What this means for decoding: after zero-phase filtering, accuracy may start rising slightly before 0 ms. With a 30 Hz cutoff this lead is very small; as long as it is only a short stretch right next to 0 ms, it is caused by the filter and is normal. If accuracy is clearly above chance around −200 ms, that is what really signals a problem in the data processing.

8The sampling theorem and aliasing

The sampling theorem (the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem) says: to record a signal correctly with discrete samples, the sampling rate must be greater than twice the signal's highest frequency.

fs > 2fmax

fs is the sampling rate (samples per second), and fmax is the highest frequency component in the signal. fs/2 is called the Nyquist frequency, the highest frequency this sampling rate can represent correctly.

Sampling rateNyquist frequencyCorrectly represents
1024 Hz (raw data)512 HzBelow 512 Hz
100 Hz (after downsampling)50 HzBelow 50 Hz

What happens if it is violated: components above the Nyquist frequency do not disappear; they masquerade as a lower frequency, which is called aliasing. When sampling is too sparse, the sampled points joined together look like a much slower wave:

falias = | f − k · fs |

f is the true frequency and k is the integer nearest to f/fs. In other words: subtract the nearest integer multiple of the sampling rate from the true frequency, then take the absolute value.

Once aliased, this noise mixes with the genuine low-frequency signal and can never be separated again. So before downsampling, a low-pass must first remove the components above the Nyquist frequency; this is called anti-aliasing filtering.
