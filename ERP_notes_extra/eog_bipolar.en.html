Bipolar EOG

(Translated by Claude Opus 5.5)

One preprocessing step marks the 3 eye channels as EOG and then computes the bipolar EOG signals HEOG = left − right and VEOG = VEOG_lower − FP2. This page explains where the EOG signal comes from, why we subtract two electrodes, and what the result is used for. All three figures are interactive.

1Where the EOG channels are and what they record

The ERP CORE N170 data have 33 channels: 30 scalp EEG channels plus 3 electrooculogram (EOG) channels [1]. The EOG channels are not there to study the brain; they are dedicated to recording eye movements.

ChannelLocationWhat it records
HEOG_left / HEOG_rightNext to the outer corner of the left / right eyeHorizontal eye movements (looking left, looking right)
VEOG_lowerBelow the right eyeBlinks and vertical eye movements. Used as a pair with the scalp electrode FP2 above the eye
FP2On the forehead, above the right eyeItself a scalp EEG channel, not a dedicated EOG electrode. Because it sits right above the eye, it is borrowed and paired with VEOG_lower to compute the bipolar VEOG

We need them because the electrical signals produced by blinks and eye movements are much larger than EEG, and they spread to the scalp electrodes, especially over the forehead.

2The eye itself is a dipole

The cornea at the front of the eyeball is positively charged relative to the retina at the back, so each eye acts like a small battery with its positive pole facing forward. When the eye rotates, the positive pole turns toward one side, and the voltage at the surrounding electrodes changes accordingly.

When looking left, the cornea (positive pole) moves closer to the left electrode: the left side becomes positive and the right side negative. Looking right does the opposite. The two sides change in opposite directions, so after subtracting right from left the eye-movement signal is twice as large as on one side, and the sign of HEOG tells you directly which way the eyes turned.

During a blink the eyelid slides over the cornea, and the electrodes above and below the eye also change in opposite directions (above becomes positive, below becomes negative). So VEOG is computed as "below − above", and a blink shows up as a large negative spike.

3Why subtract two electrodes

A single EOG electrode records its voltage relative to the recording reference. Besides eye movements, this contains brain activity and various kinds of common noise (e.g. 60 Hz line noise, slow drift). These components are nearly identical at the electrodes on both sides of the eyes (after all, the two eyes are roughly symmetric). Let the left and right electrodes record:

left = e + c　　　right = −e + c　　　left − right = 2e

e is the voltage that eye movement produces at the electrode, and c is the component shared by both sides (brain activity, common noise). Because we assume the participant is not cross-eyed, the eye-movement voltages of the two eyes are consistent. After subtraction c cancels and e doubles.

Turn up the common noise and the single-electrode waveforms quickly become too messy to see the eye movements; the bipolar EOG is almost unaffected, and the left look at 0.5 s, the blink at 1.0 s and the right look at 1.4 s all remain clear. Note that the blink is almost invisible in HEOG but large in VEOG; the left/right looks are the reverse. The two bipolar EOG signals have clearly divided roles:

Bipolar EOGHow it is computedMainly showsDirection
HEOGHEOG_left − HEOG_rightLeft/right eye movementsPositive when looking left, negative when looking right
VEOGVEOG_lower − FP2Blinks, up/down eye movementsA blink is a large negative spike

4What the result is used for

The main use is rejecting trials. If the participant is blinking or moving their eyes at the moment the stimulus appears, they may not have seen the image properly, and the EOG itself contaminates that stretch of EEG, so the trial should be removed. This is also what the official ERP CORE pipeline does [1]. The method is simple: during the stimulus presentation window, check whether the bipolar HEOG and VEOG exceed a threshold.

Figure 3 · Rejecting trials by bipolar VEOG (10 simulated trials)
Kept / rejected

The shaded area is the stimulus presentation window (illustrative), and the red dashed line is the threshold. Trials that exceed the threshold inside the shaded area are removed; trials whose blink falls outside the shaded area are kept. A threshold that is too low also removes normal trials, while one that is too high lets real blinks through. The official ERP CORE thresholds were set per participant by inspecting the data; in this exercise we use a single uniform threshold for simplicity.

5Why must the EOG channel type be marked during preprocessing?

When the data are loaded into MNE, all 33 channels are treated as EEG by default. Once the 3 eye channels are marked as eog, MNE knows they are not brain signals: when computing the average reference they are not included in the average over all electrodes; they do not appear in scalp topography plots; and in decoding they are not used as features.

The last point is especially important for decoding. Participants may move their eyes differently when viewing faces versus cars, for example looking more often at the location of the eyes in the image when viewing faces. If the EOG is included as a feature, the classifier may well separate the two classes from eye movements alone, giving accuracy that looks high but has nothing to do with the brain. That is why we use only the 30 scalp EEG channels for decoding.
