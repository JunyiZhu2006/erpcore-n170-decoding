Lagrange Multipliers and Support Vectors
The math behind SVM · Part 1

Lagrange multipliers: why only the support vectors count

(Translated by Claude Opus 5.5)

Yesterday we said that SVM wants to minimise ‖w‖ subject to "every point lies outside its dashed line". This kind of "minimisation with conditions" is solved with Lagrange multipliers. This page first makes the idea clear with a small 2D problem, then comes back to SVM. Every figure can be dragged.

0First, the symbol ∇: the gradient

You will see ∇f again and again below, read as "the gradient of f" (the symbol ∇ is called nabla). It simply lines up the derivatives of f with respect to each variable into a vector.

  1. One variable: the derivative is the slope. f(x) = x², derivative f′(x) = 2x. At x = 1 the slope is 2: nudge x a tiny bit to the right, and f grows by about 2 times that nudge.
  2. Two variables: partial derivatives. f(x, y) = x² + y² has two variables, so the slope depends on direction. Move only x and treat y as a constant: ∂f/∂x = 2x, the partial derivative with respect to x. Move only y: ∂f/∂y = 2y. ∂ is the partial-derivative symbol; you compute it like an ordinary derivative, just treating the other variables as constants.
  3. Stack the partial derivatives into a vector, and that is the gradient.
∇f = ∂f/∂x∂f/∂y = 2x2y
At point∇fMeaning
(1, 1)22Points up and to the right, away from the origin
(2, 0)40Points straight right; farther from the origin, so the arrow is longer
(0, 0)00Bottom of the bowl: no direction gets steeper right away, so the gradient is zero

The gradient has two geometric meanings, and everything below relies on them:

  • Direction: it points where f increases fastest. f = x² + y² is like a bowl, and the gradient always points away from the origin, up the wall of the bowl.
  • Length: the rate of change of f at this point along the steepest direction. The farther from the origin, the steeper the bowl and the longer the gradient. More on this below.

"Length = rate of change" comes in two cases

The gradient's length tells you: walk a distance d along the gradient, and f increases by about ‖∇f‖ × d. That "about" behaves very differently for two kinds of function. Both figures start at a point and walk along the gradient; the small chart on the right plots the actual increase together with the increase predicted from the gradient's length.

Difference

A linear function is like a tilted plane, equally steep everywhere, so its contours are evenly spaced parallel lines: neighbouring lines differ by 1 and are all 0.2 = 1/5 apart. However far you walk, the actual increase is exactly 5 × d, and the two lines on the right coincide. Walking 0.2 reaches the f = 1 contour; walking 1 reaches f = 5.

Difference

The bowl gets steeper the farther out you go, so its contour circles crowd closer and closer together. Drag d very small (say 0.05) and the actual increase almost matches the prediction; the farther you walk, the more the actual increase exceeds the prediction, and the curve on the right bends up away from the straight line. Here the difference is exactly d²: actual increase = 2.83d + d².

So the gradient's length is the rate of change of f at this point along the steepest direction, the same idea as a car's speed: a speedometer showing 60 km/h means that at the current speed you would cover 60 km in an hour, but the speed may change the next second. A linear function has constant speed, so the prediction is always right; a curved function is only accurate over a very small step.

One more property: the gradient is always perpendicular to the contour lines. Walk along a contour and f does not change; walk along the gradient and f rises fastest. These two directions are perpendicular to each other.

1The problem: finding a minimum under a condition

Start with a problem much simpler than SVM:

minimise f(x, y) = x² + y² subject to: x + y = 2

f is just the squared distance from the point to the origin. Without the condition, the minimum is obviously at the origin. But the condition requires the point to stay on the line x + y = 2, so the question becomes: which point on the line is closest to the origin?

The circles in the figure are contours of f: points on the same circle have the same f, and f grows outward. The purple line is the constraint. Drag the slider to move the point along the line and watch how f changes.

The red arrow is the gradient of f, ∇f, pointing where f rises fastest. The purple arrow is the constraint's gradient ∇g, perpendicular to the line.

2Key observation: at the optimum the two arrows are parallel

Drag to where f is smallest (the point (1, 1)) and you will see two things happen at once: the contour circle just touches the line, and the red and purple arrows point in the same direction.

Why must they be parallel? If the red arrow ∇f is not perpendicular to the line, part of it lies along the line. Then moving a tiny bit along the line in the opposite direction makes f smaller, so this is not yet the minimum. Only when ∇f is fully perpendicular to the line, i.e. parallel to ∇g, does moving along the line in either direction fail to make f smaller.

Written as an equation, "parallel" means one arrow is a multiple of the other:

∇f = λ · ∇g

This multiple λ (read "lambda") is the Lagrange multiplier. It is just a number, telling you "at the optimum, the objective's gradient is this many times the constraint's gradient".

3What λ means, and inequality constraints

λ is not just a helper number; it has a concrete meaning: how much the optimal value gets worse if the constraint is tightened a little. At the optimum (1, 1) of Figure 1, ∇f = 22 is exactly 2 times ∇g = 11, so λ = 2. That means: if the condition changes from x + y = 2 to x + y = 2.1, the minimum rises by about 2 × 0.1 = 0.2.

SVM's constraints are inequalities ("score at least 1"), so now look at x + y ≥ c: the point only needs to be on the line or above and to the right of it. Two situations can arise; drag c to see:

Both cases together can be written as λ · (x + y − c) = 0: either λ is 0, or the point is exactly on the boundary. This is called complementary slackness, and SVM's support vectors come straight out of this statement.

  1. Objective: make the margin as wide as possible while satisfying all conditions.
margin = 2 / ‖w‖, ‖w‖ = √(w₁² + w₂²)

‖w‖ is the length of w; in Figure 3, ‖w‖ = √(1.19² + 0.92²) ≈ 1.51. Below we derive why the margin is 2/‖w‖.

Derivation · margin = 2 / ‖w‖

Setup

  • Boundary (solid line): points with score 0, i.e. wᵀx + b = 0
  • Dashed line: points with score 1, i.e. wᵀx + b = 1 (the other one is −1)
  • margin = perpendicular distance between the two dashed lines = 2 × (perpendicular distance from a dashed line to the solid line)

So we only need the perpendicular distance d from a dashed line to the solid line.

Step 1: the direction perpendicular to the boundary

The partial derivative of the scoring function with respect to x₁ is w₁, and with respect to x₂ is w₂, so its gradient is w. The gradient is perpendicular to contours (Section 0), and the boundary is the "score = 0" contour, so w is perpendicular to the boundary. Shrink it to a unit-length direction vector:

u = w‖w‖

u is the direction "perpendicular to the boundary, with length 1".

Step 2: start at a point on the dashed line and walk d along −u

Take any point x₀ on the dashed line; it satisfies wᵀx₀ + b = 1. Walk a distance d in the −u direction (toward the boundary) and you arrive at

x₀ − d·u

Step 3: compute the new point's score

wT(x₀ − d·u) + b = (wTx₀ + b)= 1, since x₀ is on the dashed line − d · wTw‖w‖

And wᵀw = w₁² + w₂² = ‖w‖², so

new point's score = 1 − d · ‖w‖²‖w‖ = 1 − d‖w‖

Meaning: the score starts at 1 and drops by ‖w‖ for every unit walked.

Step 4: require reaching the boundary

We want to know how far to walk to reach the boundary. The boundary is where the score equals 0, so set the "new point's score" from Step 3 to 0. With numbers: d = 1/1.51 ≈ 0.66, matching where the score reaches 0 in the readout. There is one dashed line on each side, so

The smaller ‖w‖, the wider the margin, so the objective is:

subject to the 14 conditions above, find the w and b that minimise ‖w‖

So SVM is the same kind of thing as the small problem: finding a minimum under inequality conditions. The only difference is that there is no longer 1 condition but "1 per data point". Side by side:

Why w is built from the support vectors

When training an SVM, the coordinates of all 14 points are known data, and what we are looking for is w₁, w₂, b. So switch viewpoint here: w₁, w₂, b are the variables, and the point coordinates are constants. Write the coordinates of point i as xᵢ = xᵢ₁xᵢ₂ (xᵢ₁ is its voltage at PO7, xᵢ₂ at PO8), and its Lagrange multiplier as αᵢ.

Equivalent objective function. The goal is to minimise ‖w‖ = √(w₁² + w₂²), but the square root is awkward to differentiate. Length is never negative, so minimising ‖w‖ and minimising ‖w‖² find the same w; we therefore minimise

F = ½‖w‖² = ½(w₁² + w₂²)

We call F the equivalent objective function: it gives exactly the same result as "widest margin", just easier to differentiate. The ½ in front is there to cancel the 2 when differentiating. It has the same shape as x² + y² from Section 1: view each pair of weights (w₁, w₂) as a point in the "weight plane", and F is half its squared distance to the origin. So SVM asks: with all 14 conditions satisfied, find the w closest to the origin.

What we need is the "parallel gradients" idea from Section 2. First write out the three kinds of function, take partial derivatives with respect to w₁ and w₂, and stack them vertically to get the gradient with respect to w:

What the objective F is: making margin = 2/‖w‖ widest means making ‖w‖ = √(w₁² + w₂²) smallest. The square root is awkward to differentiate, and minimising ‖w‖ or ‖w‖² gives the same w, so we use the square; the ½ in front only cancels the 2 when differentiating. It has the same shape as x² + y² from Section 1: in the (w₁, w₂) plane, F is half the squared distance to the origin. So SVM is finding the w closest to the origin while satisfying the 14 conditions.

In the score xᵢ₁w₁ + xᵢ₂w₂ + b, the point's coordinates are the coefficients of w₁ and w₂, so the partial derivatives with respect to w leave exactly the coordinates. Class A has an extra minus sign because "≤ −1" was flipped into "≥ 1".

Section 2 had only one condition, and the optimum satisfied ∇f = λ∇g. With several conditions this becomes gradient of the objective = each condition's gradient times its own multiplier, all added up:

∇F = Σi αi ∇Gi

Intuition: the optimum is pressed against several boundaries at once, and the objective's gradient must be expressible as a combination of those boundaries' directions; otherwise you could still slide a little along the boundaries in some direction and make the objective smaller. Substituting the three gradients from the table:

w = Σclass B αi xi − Σclass A αi xi

Now use the conclusion of Section 3: a point not pressed against its dashed line has αᵢ = 0, so its term is the zero vector and adding it changes nothing. Only the support vectors actually remain, and that is the mathematical meaning of "the boundary is determined only by the support vectors".

Figure · building w from the three support vectors of Figure 3
Running sum

The other 11 points have α = 0; each of their terms is the zero vector, which draws as just a dot and does not change the result.

Each term is "α × coordinates", with a plus sign for class B and a minus sign for class A. Placed head to tail, the three terms end exactly at the w computed by sklearn.

While we are at it, take the partial derivative with respect to b. The objective F does not contain b, so its partial derivative with respect to b is 0; for class B, Gᵢ has partial derivative +1 with respect to b, and for class A it is −1. By the same "parallel gradients" rule:

0 = Σclass B αi − Σclass A αi

That is, the α values of class A add up to the same total as the α values of class B. In Figure 3: 0.618 + 0.514 = 1.132, exactly point 13's α. This condition is why, as Section 5 says, SMO must adjust two α values at a time.

This also answers a question raised earlier: why is w perpendicular to the boundary? Take the partial derivative of the scoring rule with respect to PO7 and you get the PO7 weight; with respect to PO8, the PO8 weight. So the gradient of the scoring rule is w itself. The boundary is the "score = 0" contour, and the gradient is always perpendicular to contours (Section 0), so w is perpendicular to the boundary. The scoring rule is also linear (Case 1 in Section 0), so walking along w, the score grows at a constant rate equal to the length of w.

5The remaining step: the dual problem and SMO

Since w can be built from the α values, we no longer need to search for w directly; instead we search for those 14 α values. The problem rephrased as "find α" is called the dual problem; it has exactly the same answer as the original problem, just with a different set of unknowns. The SMO algorithm mentioned yesterday solves this problem.

The dual problem has one condition: the α values of all class A points must add up to the same total as those of all class B points. You can check it in Figure 3: class A gives 0.618 + 0.514 = 1.132, class B gives 1.132, exactly equal. Change only one α and the two sides no longer match, so SMO must adjust at least two α values at a time, one changing and the other compensating. That is why it "picks two at a time".

The soft-margin version (the one with parameter C) adds only one condition: no α may exceed C. In other words, each point's influence is capped, so points on the wrong side cannot pull the boundary without limit.

✓In one sentence

Lagrange multipliers are a method for "minimising under conditions": at the optimum, the objective's gradient is parallel to the constraint's gradient, and the multiple is the multiplier. With inequality constraints, a constraint that is not pressed against its boundary has multiplier 0. SVM gives each data point a constraint; the points pressed against the dashed lines have multipliers greater than 0, they are the support vectors, and the boundary is determined by them alone.
